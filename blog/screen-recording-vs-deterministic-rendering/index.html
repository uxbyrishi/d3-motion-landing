1. The OS Display Scaling Downsample Trap

Most modern laptops and monitors run with display scaling enabled:

  • MacBook Retina screens scale by 2.0x (200%) or virtual fractional scales like 1.75x.
  • Windows 4K laptops typically default to 150% or 175% scaling.

When you use OBS or QuickTime to capture a window on your desktop, the recorder grabs the already-scaled desktop framebuffer. If you drag the video into an editor or upload it to social media, the video undergoes a secondary resampling stage.

Subpixel text rendering (ClearType or macOS subpixel antialiasing) is optimized specifically for your physical display. When screen recorded and recompressed into H.264 video, subpixel color fringing smears into noticeable gray blur.

2. Variable Frame Rate (VFR) Stutter

When you record your screen, your computer is performing two heavy operations simultaneously on the exact same GPU:

  1. Executing JavaScript, CSS transitions, and WebGL shader passes in your browser.
  2. Capturing, downsampling, and encoding desktop video frames into an MP4 container.

The moment your web animation hits a heavy cubic-bezier calculation, the browser thread starves the video encoder. The encoder simply skips frames or logs a Variable Frame Rate (e.g. 43.2fps). On social media platforms that force constant 60fps playback, this produces noticeable micro-jitters.

3. How Deterministic Virtual Rendering Solves It

D3motion does not record your physical screen. Instead:

  • Virtual Headless Viewport: Your HTML/CSS animation is mounted inside a dedicated virtual viewport at exact target dimensions (e.g. 1920×1080 or 3840×2160).
  • 1:1 Native Pixel Mapping: Zero OS scaling interference. Every pixel in the CSS coordinate space maps directly to an H.264 macroblock.
  • Strict Constant Frame Rate (CFR): Each frame advances the virtual clock by exactly 16.666ms. It waits until rendering is 100% complete before advancing.
